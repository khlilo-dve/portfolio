---

Most of us are constantly, unconsciously, making action judgments on repeat:

Should I do this thing today?

Should I buy this cup of coffee?

Is this product really the best deal?

You think your exhaustion comes from grinding day after day. But what actually wears you out isn't the work itself — it's the endless internal deliberation and friction along the way.

Your judgment is a far scarcer resource than your execution. And this precious judgment should be reserved for genuine directional choices. Everything else needs to get out of the way.

What is a "judgment question"? It's something without a clear answer yet — something that requires you to fire up your judgment to weigh. "Should I take a cab?" "Should I take on this project?" "Should I try this from one more angle?" What is a "fill-in-the-blank question"? It's something where the framework is locked in, the output standard is explicit, and the only thing left to do is fill in the content. A thousand screws left to tighten? You keep tightening. You don't stop to wonder, "Maybe I should switch wrenches."

Once you break down life's actions into these two types, a brutal truth emerges: most people's "work" is really just doing judgment questions all day. You think you're executing, but before every execution there's a five-second hesitation, a comparison, three minutes of self-doubt — all of it draining your judgment reserves.

Since it's a judgment, there are only two outcomes: yes or no. Either commit firmly, or don't care.

For the big directional choices: once chosen, you enter a state of mechanical execution on that path. For everything else: adopt an attitude of complete indifference —

Should I spend 30 bucks on a cab this time? Don't care.

Did I make the school's award list this time? Don't care.

My coworkers had a gathering and didn't invite me — am I being excluded? Don't care.

"Don't care" doesn't mean you can handle the consequences of a choice. It means the consequences are all the same to you.

Derek Sivers has a brutally uncompromising framework: something either makes you so excited you say **Hell Yes**, or it's an explicit **Fucking No**. There is no middle ground.

But what's reality? Most people make twenty judgment calls a day, and eighteen of them are neither Hell Yes nor Fucking No — they're "it's alright," "let's give it a shot," "what if it's useful?" You follow these judgments, and the result is a continuous, subtle drift in direction.

A tiny deviation each time, another step forward, another tiny deviation, another step. You think you're moving in a straight line, but draw it out and it's a spiral — circling right back to where you started.

Luo Yonghao once said something that was originally criticizing a poverty mindset, but it applies perfectly here: it's not that your time is worthless so you save ten bucks — it's that precisely because you keep saving ten bucks, your entire life operates at the "save ten bucks" level. Saving ten bucks itself isn't the problem. The problem is the three minutes before checkout where you ran three micro-judgments: "which store?" "should I use a coupon?" "would it be cheaper if I wait for Black Friday?" Their cost isn't additive — it's erosive. They eat away at the judgment you could have allocated to your main quest.

Continuous directional drift is the root reason most people never produce real results. It's not that you didn't work — it's that every bit of work you did pointed in a slightly different direction.

So the question isn't "how do I try harder?" It's: how do you allocate your judgment like a master?

**Big decisions: single-minded. Hell Yes or Fucking No.** This one principle alone kills the vast majority of middle-ground hesitation. Anything you've placed in the "not sure if I should do this" pile is, by definition, a Fucking No. The fact that you're hesitating means it hasn't even cleared the excitement threshold. What you need to do isn't think more — it's cut it outright and save that judgment. There are hard battles ahead.

**Small decisions: turn your brain off entirely.** Tim Ferriss eats the exact same breakfast every morning — not for health, but to completely remove the micro-judgment of "what to eat" from his life. The moment you start rationalizing "should I take a cab," you've already lost. It's not about whether you should or shouldn't — it's that the instant you start proving to yourself why you should or shouldn't, you're manufacturing an unnecessary judgment question and burning resources that don't belong to this domain.

Don't care.

These three words are the strongest judgment shield there is. Take a cab, don't take a cab — emotionally, there's no difference. Its opposite is: you save ten bucks and feel a subtle sense of "I saved"; you fail to save and feel like you lost. That emotional fluctuation is evidence of your judgment being scattered.

Lock in big decisions with a minimalist principle. Block out small decisions with the "don't care" mindset. This one tightening, one releasing — it serves a single purpose: channel all mental energy into the main quest, so that on the main quest there are no more judgments, only execution.

The path to mastery is a life composed of fewer and fewer judgment questions and more and more fill-in-the-blank questions.

You go from being led by the nose by countless small judgments in your youth, to understanding that judgment is a finite resource and proactively cutting out the middle ground, to ultimately — making only a handful of life-reshaping big judgments, then pouring the remaining decades into massive-scale fill-in-the-blank execution.

Naval has a lion-like hunting logic: lock onto one point and never let go. Maslow's pyramid was never about climbing step by step from the bottom — once a certain layer is satisfied, any layer becomes freely available to you. What it gives you isn't a sequence, but a space. If you want to stand on the self-actualization layer and go all out, all you need to do is compress the lower layers into fill-in-the-blank questions as fast as possible, and reserve all your judgment for the top.

This is how masters are forged: direction is extremely firm, judgments are extremely few, execution is extremely tedious and massive. Look at those wealthy people, those who live with clarity — they grind fourteen hours a day like it's play. Are they tired? No. Not because they have superior stamina, but because they have zero internal friction. All their mental energy pours directly into concrete work. They live clean.

As an ordinary person, whether you can do this doesn't depend on whether you want to — it depends on whether you can. Before you've completed your main quest, you have no business wondering "is there a better opportunity over there?" or "is that person attractive?" When there are a thousand screws still to tighten, the foreman isn't asking how you feel, and you shouldn't have feelings either.

As long as you maintain the "don't care" attitude, save your mental energy, and pour it all into the main path you've chosen — stick with it for five years, ten years. While others haven't even started working, you've done an astronomical amount of work. It's impossible not to produce results.

At that point, the feeling of mental exhaustion will disappear. Because you're no longer choosing.
